Arranger's tips (from me, Day 12)

🎵 A tooth can't hold a note. It rings, then fades. For a long note, real music box arrangers put in a quick row of repeats: tink-tink-tink. Listen to the train whistle in "Biscuit's Chuff".

🧈 Low teeth ring longer (they're long and have lead weights under them). Put a bass note on the first beat and let it ring.

🤫 Dampers: if you pin the same tooth twice in a row, the little damper wire quiets the first ring right before the next pluck. No buzzing.

🪭 The fly is the tiny fan on top of the endless screw. Air pushes back harder the faster it spins (twice as fast = 4 times the push), so the spring can't rush. Bigger vanes = more push = a slower tune. Try it!

How a music box works

In 1796 a Geneva clockmaker, Antoine Favre, swapped a stack of little bells for a comb of tuned steel teeth. A brass cylinder full of pins turns past it, and each pin plucks one tooth. Factories make each tooth ring, compare it to the right note, and grind a little off until it's in tune. Real cylinders get their pins one by one on a pinning lathe from a plan made by an arranger (that's you now!). A big 72-note cylinder can have over 1,200 pins. Then they fill the inside with warm resin so the pins don't wiggle.

A changeante box slides its cylinder sideways a hair after each turn so a different row of pins lines up with the teeth. Mine does that: 3 tunes on one cylinder.
